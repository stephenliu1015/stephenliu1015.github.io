Topic 1: Access Control & the Principle of Least Privilege

Principle: Confidentiality & Integrity

My Understanding

Access control decides who can use which resources and what they are allowed to do with them. It rests on three steps: identification (claiming an identity), authentication (proving it), and authorization (granting only the permissions that identity needs). The principle of least privilege, first described by Saltzer and Schroeder (1975), says every user and process should operate with the smallest set of privileges required for the job. That limits the damage when an account is compromised or a mistake is made.

What this course helped me see is that least privilege is not a one-time setting but a lifecycle. Permissions tend to accumulate as people change roles ("privilege creep"), so access has to be reviewed and removed as deliberately as it is granted. Role-based access control (RBAC) makes this manageable by attaching permissions to roles instead of to individuals, and multi-factor authentication strengthens the authentication step that everything else depends on (Grassi et al., 2017).

Examples & Applications

I apply this daily in Microsoft Entra ID. Instead of granting permissions user by user, I place people in security groups mapped to their roles, and those groups control access to SharePoint sites, mailboxes, and applications. When someone leaves or changes teams, removing them from a group revokes everything tied to that role at once. Onboarding and offboarding are where least privilege either holds or breaks down, so I treat deprovisioning with the same care as provisioning.

Topic 2: Social Engineering & the Human Factor

Principle: People, process & technology

My Understanding

Social engineering attacks target people rather than systems. Phishing, pretexting, and impersonation work by exploiting trust, urgency, fear, and authority to persuade someone to click a link, share credentials, or approve a request. Because these attacks bypass technical controls by convincing an authorized user to act, they show why security has to account for people and process, not just technology.

This course reinforced that technical controls and user awareness work best as layers of defense in depth. Email filtering blocks most malicious messages, but some will always reach an inbox, so users need to recognize warning signs and know how to report them. The goal of awareness training is not to blame users for clicking but to build habits and a reporting culture that shortens the time between an attack and a response.

Examples & Applications

At work, I run monthly phishing simulations and analyze how users respond: who clicks, who enters credentials, and, just as important, who reports the message. I use those results to deliver targeted security awareness guidance. I also investigate suspicious messages with Exchange Online message traces and manage spam filtering through Barracuda Email Security, so I see both the technical and human layers of the same defense.

Topic 3: Risk & Vulnerability Management

Principle: Availability & risk-based decisions

My Understanding

Risk is a combination of the likelihood that a threat exploits a vulnerability and the impact if it does (National Institute of Standards and Technology [NIST], 2012). Since no organization can eliminate every vulnerability, risk management is about prioritizing: identifying assets, assessing threats and weaknesses, and then choosing to mitigate, transfer, accept, or avoid each risk. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 organizes this work into six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover (NIST, 2024).

Patch management is one of the most practical forms of risk reduction, because many attacks exploit known vulnerabilities that already have fixes available. The challenge is balancing security against availability: patches need to be tested and scheduled so they close vulnerabilities without disrupting the business (Souppaya & Scarfone, 2022).

Examples & Applications

As a Tier 2 Help Desk Technician, I helped raise Windows patch compliance across client environments from 82% to 97% through proactive update management and remediation of devices that were falling behind. Today I enforce security baselines and compliance policies through Microsoft Intune, which turns risk decisions into consistent, automated controls across every managed device.

Project / Research Showcase

Microsoft 365 Security Assessment of a Small-Business Environment

Applying CYSE 600 principles to evaluate the security of a typical small-business Microsoft 365 tenant and recommend risk-prioritized improvements.

Overview

Question: How well does a typical small-business Microsoft 365 environment protect confidentiality, integrity, and availability, and which improvements would reduce the most risk for the least effort?

Scope: A representative 50-user tenant modeled on the kinds of environments I support as a Support Engineer. No real client data is used. The assessment covers identity and access, email security, endpoint management, and data sharing.

Method: I reviewed the environment against the CIS Critical Security Controls (Center for Internet Security, 2021) and NIST guidance, mapped each finding to the CIA triad, and rated it using the likelihood × impact approach from NIST SP 800-30 (NIST, 2012). Recommendations were then organized by the NIST CSF 2.0 functions (NIST, 2024).

Findings & Recommendations

Remediation Roadmap

Phase 1, first 30 days (Protect): enforce MFA, block legacy authentication, and reduce Global Administrators. Phase 2, 60 days (Identify & Protect): fix offboarding, run the first access review, and complete Intune enrollment. Phase 3, 90 days (Detect & Govern): enforce DMARC, tighten sharing defaults, and add these checks to a recurring monthly review.

Reflection & Analysis

I chose a Microsoft 365 security assessment because it sits where my coursework and my job meet. Every day I configure parts of this environment one ticket at a time, but I had never stepped back to evaluate it as a whole system. This project asked a different question than my job usually does: not "how do I fix this?" but "how secure is this, and what should be fixed first?" Answering that required the principles from CYSE 600 rather than just technical skill.

The most important insight was how much of the environment's risk came from identity. Four of the six findings involved who could sign in and what they could do once inside: missing MFA, too many Global Administrators, stale accounts, and unmanaged devices reaching company data. This connects directly to the principle of least privilege (Saltzer & Schroeder, 1975). Each excess privilege is not a problem until an account is compromised, and then it determines how far an attacker can go. A compromised standard user is an incident; a compromised Global Administrator can mean losing control of the entire tenant. Seeing the findings side by side made it clear that identity is the real perimeter in a cloud environment.

Rating the findings with likelihood and impact (NIST, 2012) changed how I would prioritize work. Without a framework, it is easy to fix whatever is most visible or easiest. A risk matrix forces the question of what would cause the most harm. Enforcing MFA and reducing administrator accounts ranked highest because both are likely targets and high impact, and both are relatively inexpensive to fix. That combination of high risk reduction and low cost is exactly what a small business with limited IT resources needs. Lower-priority items, such as sharing defaults, still matter, but they belong in a later phase.

Mapping each finding to the CIA triad also exposed something I had underestimated: availability. I usually think of security as protecting confidentiality, but unpatched devices and over-privileged accounts threaten availability too, through ransomware or a malicious administrator deleting data. The roadmap reflects that by treating patch compliance as a security control, not just routine maintenance, consistent with NIST's view of patching as preventive maintenance (Souppaya & Scarfone, 2022).

The assessment also reinforced that technical controls alone are not enough. Several findings were really process failures. Stale accounts in security groups are not a configuration problem; they happen because offboarding is inconsistent. Likewise, MFA protects against stolen passwords, but users still need awareness training to recognize phishing attempts designed to steal MFA codes or trick them into approving sign-in prompts. That is why the roadmap pairs technical changes with recurring reviews and procedures. Defense in depth means layering people, process, and technology so that one failure does not lead to a breach.

This project has limitations. The environment is a composite based on my experience rather than a live audit, so the findings illustrate common patterns instead of measuring one organization. A real assessment would also include log analysis, interviews with staff, and testing of whether controls actually work as configured. Another limitation is that the risk ratings involve judgment; two analysts could reasonably rate the same finding differently. Documenting the reasoning behind each rating, as I did by mapping findings to the CIA triad and NIST guidance, makes those judgments transparent and open to review.

Professionally, this project gave me a repeatable method I can bring to work. Instead of only resolving tickets, I can periodically evaluate client environments against a framework such as the CIS Controls (Center for Internet Security, 2021), rank what I find, and present the results in terms leadership understands: risk, cost, and priority. That ability to translate technical issues into business decisions is what separates IT support from security work, and it is the step I want to take next in my career.

Learning Reflections

What I Learned

CYSE 600 gave me the theory behind the work I already do. Before this course, I knew how to configure access controls, filter email, and push patches. Now I understand why those controls matter, how they fit together as layers of defense, and how to explain security decisions in terms of risk.

1. My Growth & Development

Coming into the program, my security knowledge was mostly hands-on and tool-specific. I held my Security+ certification and worked in Microsoft 365 every day, but I tended to think in terms of individual settings rather than principles. This course changed that. Concepts like least privilege, the CIA triad, and defense in depth gave me a framework for connecting tasks that used to feel separate. Managing security groups in Entra ID, running phishing simulations, and enforcing Intune baselines are all expressions of the same goal: reducing risk to confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

My Core Knowledge artifacts show this shift. The RBAC diagram and risk matrix are ways of explaining my daily work to someone who has never seen an admin console, which is a skill I did not have before this semester.

2. A Challenge I Overcame

My biggest challenge was moving from practical, technical thinking to academic analysis. At work, success means a ticket is resolved. In this course, I had to explain the reasoning behind a control, support it with frameworks like NIST guidance, and consider trade-offs such as security versus usability. My first assignments described what to do but did not explain why it worked or cite sources well.

To work through this, I started reading frameworks and course material alongside my own work experience, asking which principle each task I did at work demonstrated. I also built a routine to balance a full-time job with graduate coursework by setting aside fixed study blocks each week. As a result, my writing became more analytical, and I now find it easier to justify security recommendations to both technical and non-technical audiences.

3. Applying What I've Learned

Professionally, I am already using this course in my role as a Support Engineer. I plan to formalize periodic access reviews for security groups to catch privilege creep, and to use risk-based reasoning when deciding which patches and compliance issues to prioritize. My phishing simulation reports will also focus more on reporting rates and on improving the overall security culture, not just click rates.

Long term, these principles support my goal of moving from IT operations into a dedicated security role such as security analyst or cloud security engineer. Academically, CYSE 600 is the foundation for the rest of my M.S. in Cybersecurity at ODU. I plan to build on it with advanced coursework in cyber defense and policy and to pursue further certifications in cloud and identity security.
